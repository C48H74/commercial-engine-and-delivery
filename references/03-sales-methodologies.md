# 3. Sales methodologies

> All four share the same purpose: **discover the truth before presenting a solution**.
> Choose by stage, not by personal preference.

| Stage | Method |
|---|---|
| Initial discovery | **SPIN** (§3.1) |
| Qualifying a complex deal | **MEDDPICC** (§3.2) |
| More than one decision-maker | **Committee map** (§3.3) |
| Client convinced but not deciding | **JOLT** (§3.4) |
| Need to create demand | **Challenger** (§3.5) |

## 3.1 SPIN — discovery in complex sales

**Author:** Neil Rackham (1988). **Basis:** research on productivity projects in companies such as Xerox, IBM and Motorola — managers trained to code calls until they agreed with standard recordings (**0.85** is the coding calibration criterion, **not** a correlation with sales results).
**When to use:** consultative sales with more than one decision-maker. **Do not** use Implication in small sales.

| Type | Goal | Example questions |
|---|---|---|
| **S**ituation | collect facts and context | "How does process X work today?" · "What tool do you use for Y?" · "How many people do Z?" |
| **P**roblem | find difficulty and dissatisfaction | "What bottlenecks do you see in process X?" · "What is your biggest difficulty with Y?" · "What complaints does the team raise about X?" |
| **I**mplication | make the client **measure** the consequence | "How does this problem affect your partners and your team?" · "Could not solving Y lead to other problems?" · "How much money do you lose each time X happens?" |
| **N**eed-payoff | the client **states** the value | "What would the ideal way of doing X look like?" · "How much would you gain in productivity if Y happened?" · "Would solving X have a significant impact on Z?" |

### Three facts from the source that change practice

1. **Implication and Need-payoff** are what move large sales. Too many Situation questions bore the buyer and only benefit the seller.
2. **The problem question has changed.** "Do you have problems with your current equipment?" now gets "no, it's fine". Buyers value the seller who asks about **future problems**. In the words of a buyer interviewed by Rackham: *"if I fall down a well, there are 50 salespeople selling me a ladder; only 1 in 50 stops me from falling."*
3. **Implication is sad; Need-payoff is happy.** Implying without offering a way out pushes the buyer down — there is a documented account of a sale lost exactly this way. **Always connect the implication to a way out.**

### Mistakes that kill SPIN
- Turning the call into an **interview** (too much Situation: value for you, not for the client).
- **Getting lost in problems** — prioritise: *"of these X things, which do you consider most important?"*
- **Presenting the solution too early** — talking about the solution early steals the problem's weight and the chance to discover the financial size.
- **Alarming too much** — the client thinks the operation is on fire and freezes.
- Believing it must be used in the order S→P→I→N. Real conversation does not follow a script: follow the client.
- Asking too many problem questions (it gives the impression you are hunting for problems at any cost).

### Golden rule
The **client** does the measuring of value. You ask; they conclude. If you concluded for them, the need remains implicit — and an implicit need does not justify investment.

### Definition of success in a large sale
There is **continuation** (permission to keep talking) and **advancement** (the client asks for more). Only advancement counts. Ending a call "well" without a committed next step is not advancement.

## 3.2 MEDDPICC — qualification of complex deals

**When to use:** large deal, multiple decision-makers, long cycle, close forecasting.

| Letter | What it is | Question for the client | Sign that it is weak |
|---|---|---|---|
| **M**etrics | quantifiable measure of value | "How do you measure success today? Which number needs to change?" | only adjectives, no number |
| **E**conomic buyer | who releases the budget | "Who has authority to approve this? Can we involve them?" | you only talk to whoever "will take it to the boss" |
| **D**ecision criteria | criteria for deciding | "What criteria will you use to choose?" | you don't know — and neither does the client: **help create them** |
| **D**ecision process | steps to signature | "What is the internal path, from yes to signature?" | unknown |
| **P**aper process | NDA, SOW, PO, MSA, legal review | "Who signs what, and how many days does that take?" | **the biggest cause of deals slipping after "yes"** |
| **I**mplicate pain | pain big enough to act on | "What happens if nothing changes this year?" | pain identified but not felt |
| **C**hampion | internal ally with power and their own interest | "If you win with this, what do you personally gain?" | a friendly contact mistaken for a champion |
| **C**ompetition | everything competing for **budget and attention** | "What else competes for this budget?" | you only thought about the direct competitor |

### Three definitions that change practice

1. **There are three "I"s.** *Identify* the pain (even a poor salesperson does this) → *indicate* the pain (quantify it as ROI or a business case — this works) → **implicate** the pain (the client **feels** it, and only that creates urgency). The chain: no pain, no problem; no problem, no urgency; no urgency, no executive sponsor; no sponsor, no budget; no budget, **no deal**.
2. **A real champion meets 3 criteria, all required:** **power and influence** (when they speak, others listen) + **sells for you when you are not in the room** + **their own interest in your success** (if you win, they win). A friendly contact is **not** a champion.
   *"No champion, no deal. Big champion, big deal."*
3. **Competition is anything fighting for the same resource:** the CIO who builds in-house, another initiative with positive ROI, a direct competitor — and above all **inertia**. Inertia is the worst because it almost always signals weak qualification.

### Decision criteria have three layers (ask about all three)
**Technical** (does it work? integrate? security? infrastructure?) · **Economic** (does the ROI add up? does it consume too many resources?) · **Relational** (do they want to work with you? does your track record inspire trust? do you work with their partners?).
Different people in the process have different criteria — **map them per person**.
In most cases the client **has no written criteria** (unless there is an RFP). So creating or influencing the criteria **is your job**.

### Rules of conduct
- **Never criticise the competitor.** Focus on your differentiators. If you set a trap, make it around your unique benefit. Clients hate criticism, and it rarely puts you ahead.
- Do a **SWOT** of the competitor **in that deal**, early. Whoever thinks about the competitor late ends up on the defensive.
- Treat MEDDPICC as a **compass**, not a form: use it to know the next step and who you have not yet met.
- **Never apply only MEDDIC** without the **Paper process** — that is where deals die after the yes.
- **Describe deals by naming the two letters where you were weakest and what you did.** That is what separates a real account from a rehearsed story.

## 3.3 Buying committee map (multithreading)

When there is **a single contact**, the risk is losing everything if they leave or change their mind.

| Role | What they decide | How to engage | Sign of absence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Executive sponsor** | releases the budget | business language, result and risk | nobody above your contact knows about the project |
| **Economic buyer** | signs the cheque | ROI, business case, cost of not acting | you have never spoken to them |
| **Key user** | uses it and can sabotage it | demonstration, time to first value | the user only appears at the end |
| **Technical blocker** | security, integration, infrastructure | objective requirements, tests | security comes up in the last week |
| **Legal / procurement** | contract, PO, NDA | advance notice, checklist, real deadlines | "legal is looking at it" with no date |
| **Champion** | sells for you | give them the business case **in their words** | your contact has no personal gain |
| **Ghost node** | nobody has mapped them | ask: *"who else needs to agree for this to move?"* | discovered late, kills the deal |

**Coverage rule:** if you only have one contact, the next action is **not** to present a proposal — it is to get a second contact.

## 3.4 JOLT — when the client is convinced but does not decide

**Author:** Matt Dixon (*The JOLT Effect*). **Basis:** machine-learning analysis of **2.5 million** recorded sales conversations — the behaviours were **observed**, not invented.
**Numbers: attributed to the author, not verified in this review.**

### The diagnosis that changes everything

There are **two** causes of "no decision":
1. **Poor status quo** — the client does not see value (the classic cause).
2. **Indecision** — the client sees value and still does not decide. **This is the bigger one:** roughly **60%** of no-decision losses are indecision, not attachment to the status quo.
   And **~87%** of the calls studied had a buyer with **moderate or high** indecision — while the seller almost never notices.

**Central insight:** increasing **FOMO** (fear of missing out) **makes things worse** — it increases the chance the client does nothing. What works is reducing **FOMU: the fear of making a mistake**.
**Why:** consolidated behavioural data — between two identical losses, one from doing nothing and one from acting and getting it wrong, people choose to do nothing. *Nobody is fired for keeping the status quo; they are fired for trying to change and failing.*

### The three sources of indecision (none of them is the status quo)

| Source | What the client thinks |
|---|---|
| **Don't know what to choose** | "the options are all good; choosing one means giving up the others" |
| **Lack of information** | "the next whitepaper / demo / report / opinion will give me the answer" |
| **Outcome uncertainty** | "I may not get the return and look bad" |

### The 4 actions — J-O-L-T

**J — Judge the level of indecision.**
Indecision is embarrassing; people will not admit it ("I am decisive"). Use **ping and echo** — send the signal and listen to the response:
> *"Many people in your sector worry about this. It's normal — we hear it all the time. I don't want to put words in your mouth, but I want to bring it to the table. If that's the case, we need to talk, because this comes up constantly."*

This names it, normalises it and opens the conversation. **Do not** use a diagnostic question ("are you indecisive?") or a process check ("can we send it to legal?").

**O — Offer your recommendation.**
Stop saying "everything here is wonderful". Recommend explicitly:
> *"Everything we do is good, you can't go wrong. But **my** recommendation is this one — and if you want something leaner, this other one also works."*

The recommendation transfers part of the responsibility for the choice to you (delegation theory): if they don't like it, it isn't only their fault. **This is why the recommended tier must exist** (§2.3).

**L — Limit the exploration.**
When the client asks for yet another demo, reference or piece of research, it **looks like progress — it is analysis paralysis**. Get them to stop wanting to be specialists and to trust you as their guide. The seller logs "advanced" in the CRM; it is often the opposite.

**T — Take the risk off the table.**
Exit clause, pro-rata refund, professional services included, an installation plan up to the value, an indicator roadmap with the client's success team:
> *"Let's sit down with the implementation team and map how we get from installation to value: which indicators we track, who takes part, which obstacles to expect."*

Goal: the client feels **"I won't look foolish buying this; I'll look like a hero."**

### Warning sign
A client asking for extra material = **risk of paralysis**, not progress. Do not celebrate it in the pipeline.

## 3.5 Challenger — when demand has to be created

**Authors:** Matthew Dixon and Brent Adamson, *The Challenger Sale*. **Basis:** study of thousands of salespeople; the best were **not** the relationship builders nor the hardest workers — they were the ones who **challenged the client's thinking**.

### The three letters (T-T-C)
- **Teach** — teach something new about their business.
- **Tailor** — adapt the same idea by role and by hierarchical level.
- **Take control** — lead the conversation, including on price.

### What "take control" is NOT
It is not being aggressive, pushy or a "used-car salesman". It is **professional, respectful firmness**: the middle ground between being passive (doing what the client wants) and being aggressive (doing what you want). It uses tension to get the client to rethink assumptions — to make them see that **the pain of staying the same is greater than the pain of changing**.

### The test of a good "Teach"
- ✅ The client says: **"hmm, I never thought of it that way."**
- ❌ The client says: "yes, that's what we already think too."

**Real example of reframing:** a training tool was selling *"we increase retention in the first 2 days"*. That sounds bad — but in a call centre, someone who leaves on day 2 does not cost the rest of the training. The insight reframed a number the client had never considered.

### The 5 seller profiles
Hard Worker · Relationship Builder · Lone Wolf · Problem Solver · **Challenger**.
**Counter-intuitive finding:** building relationships is the **least** effective approach for closing.
**Context:** more than **40%** of consistently high-performing salespeople fit this profile; and the buyer arrives having done **more than half** of the buying process before talking to you.

### Five steps of application
1. **Warm up** with credibility and research — **without mentioning the product**; make the client curious.
2. **Reframe** the problem as an opportunity; undo the solution idea they already had.
3. **Emotion and story** — show what happens if nothing changes.
4. **Propose a better future** — still **without mentioning the product**; let them connect the dots.
5. **Only then** present the product. If the previous steps worked, this step is quick.

### The critique — record both versions
Some argue, with reason, that: **"teaching" is too passive** ("the client learns on the internet; you are there to sell and convince"); **"tailor" is obvious** (every good salesperson adapts the conversation); **"take control" is what any good salesperson does**; and **the research is fragile** when it becomes a manual ("beware of someone who has never sold telling you how to sell").
**Use it with awareness:** it works best in complex B2B where demand must be **created**, not in replacement sales or with every type of salesperson.

## 3.6 How to choose in 30 seconds

| Situation | Method | First question to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Know nothing about the client yet | SPIN | "how does process X work today?" |
| Know the pain, not sure it's a deal | MEDDPICC | "who has authority to approve this?" |
| Talking to one person only | Committee map | "who else needs to agree for this to move?" |
| Said "I'll think about it" | JOLT | ping and echo (§3.4) |
| They don't see the problem | Challenger | reframing (§3.5) |
